What I did

Building it from the orders

Two sources, for two different jobs. Where things go came from VOLO's corpus: 160 load orders that people arranged themselves (not VOLO) and reported as played on, 61,561 mod placements, and the section headers those people wrote by hand. For each family I took the median position in the order as a fraction (0 top, 1 bottom), the spread, and how often one family sits before another when both are present. How big things are came from the whole library rather than the corpus: 19,991 Nexus listings in 22 categories with download counts, 8,997 mod.io listings with tags. The corpus covers 11,673 mods; the library is about two and a half times that, so sizing from the corpus alone would mean sizing from the popular half.

Then the rule: hundreds in the order working orders put them, tens inside each hundred in corpus order where the corpus has a view (a pair holding at 60 percent or better on a thousand or more observations) and by theme where it does not, with each ten saying which it is.

Testing was the same measure VOLO is judged on. Every mod in every order gets a number. For every pair of mods with different numbers, did the player have them that way round? Random is 50. VOLO's own learned categories score about 73 on this. Then, because the hundreds were read off the same orders the test scores them on, I re-derived the whole order from half the corpus and scored the other half, ten times on random splits, to make sure I was not reading my own data back to myself.

What the test found

Where I was wrong, and where you were

My first draft scored exactly level with VOLO, which was mildly disappointing until I looked at why. Two hundreds were misplaced. Items against Gameplay turned out to be a dead heat, 50.1 percent on 711,331 pairs, so your Gameplay-first order stands and I had it backwards. And I had Story and World at 600, like you, and the data said no quite firmly: quests sit mid-order level with classes, dialogue edits sit near the bottom with companions, camp and environment edits sit right at the end. Three different places. Dissolving 600 and moving them where they go took the scheme two points past VOLO on the pairs both can judge, and it held when re-derived from half the corpus.

The finding that actually matters is that all of that gain comes from one thing. Gameplay, Visuals and Bug Fixes are each two or three completely different populations under one Nexus label. Cheats, difficulty and rules tweaks sit early; dialogue edits and "overhauls" sit at the bottom. Texture and lighting foundations sit near the top; character visuals sit mid to late; camp edits sit last. Plain fixes sit mid-order; patches and compatibility frameworks sit last. That is directly relevant to your 700 Visual / Audio, which has textures, animations, character customisation, photo mode and dice in one hundred, and the data says those live at opposite ends of the list.

The less flattering finding, for both of us: inside a hundred the order of the tens barely registers. 54 to 56 percent, where 50 is random. The exceptions are real and worth knowing (heads before hair is 78 percent on 218,706 pairs, feats before classes 77 percent on 33,677, compatibility frameworks last in every pair they appear in) but they are exceptions. Say in the pack that the tens are filing, and only order them where the data orders them.

The scheme

Hundreds in load order, tens inside

Each number carries its evidence. "Before X 70 percent" means that of every pair of mods with one in each family, the player had them that way round 70 percent of the time. A ten marked as theme has no corpus header behind it and sits where it does by judgement.

Filing rules

The three families Nexus lumps together

Whoever files a mod needs a rule they can apply without the spreadsheet.

Gameplay. Changes a rule, a number or a system you meet in play (rests, carry weight, difficulty, cheats, AI, trade, camp): 100. Adds a quest or restores cut content: 190. Edits dialogue, romance or banter: 760. Called an overhaul and its page says load it late: 920. Called a fix: 910.

Visuals. Textures, upscales, reshades and lighting foundations other visual mods build on: 050. Changes how a character, companion or NPC looks: 500 for your own character, 720 or 740 for theirs. World lighting, weather, environment and camp scenery: 810 or 850. Spell and effect visuals: 830.

Bug Fixes. A fix for the base game: 910. A patch that makes two mods work together: 940 if it patches a whole family, 950 if it patches one named mod. Anything with "compatibility framework" in its name or requirements: 960, and nothing after it except what its own page says.

VOLO

What it does with this now

VOLO is not sorting by it. Two points on one corpus, from a regex standing in for a human filing mods, is not enough to replace categories that learn from the data as it comes in. What it is doing, as of this week, is scoring every submitted order under this scheme alongside its own categories, on the same pairs, and logging both. Submitters see one line in their report saying it happened and that nothing about their sort changed. In a few months the log will say whether the gap is real on orders the scheme never saw. If it is, the likely outcome is not that VOLO adopts the numbering but that it takes the three-way split into its own categories, because that is where the gain lives and it needs no numbering at all.

And with your pack

Yes, and it is less work than you might think, because VOLO does not use Astra's categories internally either. It recognises Astra's divider paks by UUID and uses them as the skeleton when someone exports with dividers turned on (off by default), and it treats the headers from any pack as evidence of where a mod goes. Yours would work the same way. What I would need:

  • The UUID of every divider pak in the pack.
  • A handful of real orders built on it, submitted through VOLO, so the headers get seen. Words VOLO already knows (Classes, Races, Armour, Quests, Companions, Dice) work immediately; new ones (Looting, Vanity, Act 2) get learned from the first few submissions.
  • Never reuse a number for a different meaning. The moment that happens the numbers stop being identifiers and we are back where Astra is.
Advice

If I were building it

  1. Keep your hundreds order except for the three moves above: Resources into 000, patch frameworks out of 020 into 900, and Story, World and Characters redistributed.
  2. Split Visuals and Gameplay by what the mod does, not by Nexus category. Write the filing rule on the pack page so users file the same way you do.
  3. Order the tens only where the data orders them, and say the rest are filing. Nobody should move a mod because of a ten.
  4. Give every divider a UUID and never reuse a number. That is what makes the pack readable by tools, VOLO included.
  5. Send me the UUIDs and a couple of orders when it exists, and VOLO will read it from day one.

Everything here is yours to use, argue with or ignore. Some of the calls are close and some are not, and knowing which is the useful part.
